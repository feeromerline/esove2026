Mosquito population mixing shapes Rift Valley fever transmission across a wildlife–livestock interface

Merline Feero, Tino Pori, Peter Thompson, Bree Beechler, Anna Jolles, Morgan Bar, Erin Gorsich

Download the poster (PDF)

Abstract

Introduction and objectives

Rift Valley fever (RVF) is caused by a zoonotic arbovirus that infects domestic and wild ruminants. Between epidemics it circulates in African buffalo in Kruger National Park, and exposure has been detected in a range of wildlife species, but how RVF is transmitted across wildlife–livestock interfaces is unknown. We quantified RVF seroprevalence and incidence in buffalo and cattle, measured mosquito feeding across the park boundary, and developed a mechanistic model to investigate how the virus circulates and how mosquitoes crossing the boundary shape transmission.

Materials and methods

We studied RVF on both sides of the western boundary of Kruger National Park. Longitudinal studies of 261 buffalo inside the park and 97 cattle on communal land outside it were supplemented by cross-sectional sampling, giving 931 serum samples (512 buffalo, 419 cattle) collected between 2014 and 2024. Blood meals from mosquitoes trapped inside and outside the park were identified to host species. We built an ordinary differential equation model with buffalo inside the park, cattle outside it, and Aedes and Culex populations on each side. Mosquito life history is temperature-dependent, following published thermal performance curves, and the Aedes season and its dry-season floor are matched to field catches. Host preferences and the share of bites mosquitoes take across the fence come from the blood-meal data.

Results and discussion

Buffalo seroprevalence was 38.96% (30/77) in 2015 and 3.19% (8/251) in 2023, compared with 1.03% (1/97) and 1.78% (4/225) in cattle in 2015 and 2024. Buffalo seroconverted in every year of the longitudinal study (8, 3 and 2 animals in 2014–15, 2015–16 and 2016–17), while none of 96 susceptible cattle seroconverted in 2015–16. RVF therefore circulated in buffalo without an outbreak in cattle. Of 292 blood-fed mosquitoes analysed, blood meals came from 29 vertebrate species, and mosquitoes fed across the fence in both directions. In the model, buffalo maintained RVF with the fence sealed (2.35 new infections per 100 susceptible buffalo per year, within field estimates for free-ranging herds). Mosquitoes crossing to feed at the measured rates barely changed buffalo infection (2.35–2.57 per 100) and were the only route to cattle, whose incidence stayed below 0.05 per 100 per year, consistent with no detected seroconversions. The park lost the virus only if mosquitoes moved one way over distances beyond known flight ranges.

Conclusion

At the Kruger interface, buffalo can maintain RVF independently of cattle. Mosquitoes feeding across the park boundary are a measurable bridge to cattle, and at field-measured rates they keep cattle exposure below detection.

Model results are preliminary. Field data are unpublished (T. Pori).
